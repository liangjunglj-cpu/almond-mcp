## NW - Perimeter housing 6F (concrete flat plate)

**Lineage: Inward courtyard-house city** (ca. 2500 BCE, Indus Valley; GHA pp. [47, 48])

Mohenjo-Daro's neighborhoods turned inward: main streets ran between largely blank house walls, and dwellings - reached by alleys - faced open courtyards, the larger ones two stories with a timber upper level. Under the streets ran drains to accessible settling tanks, and the whole quarter stood on a brick flood platform; the colonnaded Great Bath at the crossing of the main streets anchored civic ritual.

*Urban role:* The courtyard block: privacy to the street, life to the interior court - with city-scale infrastructure taken seriously from the start.

## NE - Towers on podium (steel frame)

**Lineage: Early steel-frame tower** (1890-1913, United States; GHA pp. [709, 710])

The skyscraper synthesized improved steel - prefabricated frames erected with startling speed - with elevators, plumbing, heating and electricity integrated into one system; at the 1900 Paris Exposition the Americans exhibited a model whose plaster skin lifted off to reveal exactly that. Chicago's Reliance Building floated glassy floors over terra-cotta spandrels while New York's Gothic-clad Woolworth Building showed the frame dressed in old wealth.

*Urban role:* Vertical real estate: the tower as investment vehicle, services machine, and skyline advertisement at once.

## SW - Terraces (party walls + I-joists)

**Lineage: Dense roof-access settlement** (7400-3000 BCE, Anatolia; GHA pp. [28, 29])

At Catal Huyuk, rectangular flat-roofed mud-brick houses packed into a single architectural mass with no streets: circulation ran across the rooftops, and each dwelling was entered by ladder through its roof. Strikingly, party walls were doubled - every house kept its own walls - and the main room, around 5 by 6 metres with benches, ovens and plastered walls, doubled as the family shrine.

*Urban role:* The earliest lesson in extreme density: a housing fabric so continuous that the roofscape becomes the street.

## SW - Timber mid-rise (glulam)

**Lineage: Bracketed timber hall in a courtyard precinct** (7th-9th c. CE, China / Japan; GHA pp. [317, 318, 348, 349])

East Asian timber halls carry low-pitched roofs with deep eaves on dou-gong bracket sets - composite stacks of horizontal, vertical and diagonal members whose size and number encode the building's rank. At Horyu-ji the golden hall and pagoda balance each other inside a cloister instead of on an axis; at Foguang the seven-bay hall's columns divide inner and outer space in a format derived from palace architecture, the halls framed by colonnaded courtyards with north and south gates.

*Urban role:* The hall-in-courtyard: buildings composed as walled precincts of courts and gates rather than freestanding objects.

## SE - Market hall (24 m trusses)

**Lineage: Civic hall over a loggia on the piazza** (12th-13th c., Italy; GHA pp. [462])

As the popolo - artisans, guildsmen, merchants - took over Italian city government, the town hall emerged: a large upper meeting hall with big windows and a proclamation balcony facing the campo, above a ground floor left open as a loggia where craftsmen and traders worked under the city's protection. For the first time in centuries, buildings and public space were conceived as one ensemble.

*Urban role:* The civic anchor: hall + balcony + piazza as one political instrument.
